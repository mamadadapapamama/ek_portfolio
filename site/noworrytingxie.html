Objective

Start with the test every family already has

In Singapore, primary schoolers who take Chinese as their mother tongue sit tingxie — a Chinese spelling test — at least every other week. The teacher reads each word aloud, and children write it in Hanzi or Pinyin.

How it started

Before this I built Pikabook, which turned any textbook photo into study material. It worked, but the use case was too broad: parents asked, “Can't I just use GPT for this?”, and they had no reason to come back. I wanted a product with a sharper need and retention built in.

The answer was at my own dinner table. My son brings home a tingxie list every other week, and I was the one reading the words aloud and checking his strokes. As a non-native speaker, I kept having to look up whether I was even pronouncing each word right, and what it meant. A test that returns on a fixed schedule is a need families can't ignore — and a natural reason to come back.

It isn't only my problem

I assumed it was hard mainly for non-Chinese families like mine. It wasn't. Even Chinese-Singaporean parents struggle: most work full time, there's little time left to sit down and study together, and checking whether their own Chinese is right adds stress of its own. Many hand it to a tuition centre.

The goal: help children learn the Chinese words they actually need, on their own — starting with this week's tingxie list, and growing into a vocabulary builder for Chinese learners.

What's built so far

v1.1: one loop for the weekly test

What's live today covers the weekly tingxie routine — from a photo of the school list to a test the child can take alone: learn, practise, test, revise.

It's a first loop, not the finished product. Next on the roadmap: a clearer view of progress for parents who don't read Chinese, then a vocabulary builder that goes beyond Singapore primary.

01

A web beta to learn fast

June 2026
Web beta

Minimum value, not minimum features

I launched a web beta in June 2026 with no accounts, curriculum, rewards or dashboards — just the weekly job. A parent uploads a photo of the list, AI extracts the words, pinyin and test type, the parent checks it, and the child practises with a teacher-like audio test in random order.

I kept a parent in the loop on purpose: accuracy matters more than magic. The minimum value wasn't “upload a list”. It was “my child can practise tingxie without me reading every word.”

What families taught me

Real use quickly showed what v1 had missed. I shipped three fixes from parent feedback:

  1. 01Pinyin and Hanzi tests. Lists mix both answer types, so the upload flow now detects and labels each item, and practice matches the format.
  2. 02Slower stroke order. Younger children couldn't follow the animation, so I added a slower mode with longer pauses.
  3. 03Longer sentences. v1 was tuned for Primary 1. I reworked the prompts so lists from Primary 3 and up extract accurately.

02

Research: what to build, for whom

After the beta
Research

Finding where the market gap and parent needs overlap

Families were using the beta with no marketing. Before building more, I needed to know what to build next, for whom, and why us. I worked in three steps.

01 · MARKET

Map the space

I mapped language-learning, Chinese-learning and vocabulary-habit apps — Duolingo, Drops, Anki, Quizlet, Skritter, Pleco, Du Chinese and more. Each solved one piece well. No one combined real school content, handwriting and a low-friction daily routine.

02 · PEOPLE

Listen to parents

I interviewed seven parents and one adult Chinese learner. Many parents can't read Chinese well enough to tell whether their child really knows a word — a stroke slightly off looks right to them. They need to prepare anywhere, even at dinner the night before a test.

03 · FOCUS

Choose one audience

Adult hobby learners were the more interesting market to me. School-exam families had the clearer, more urgent pull. I chose the families — and kept adult learners on the roadmap for later.

What parents need

Four needs came up again and again. They became the product's four jobs:

Empower children to learn Chinese independently
Build a bite-size learning habit
Give parents a clear view of progress and what to revise
Turn the words children need into personalised practice
Vision & roadmap

From a tingxie tool to a Chinese vocabulary builder

I rewrote the vision around those four jobs: “A world where every learner feels confident using Chinese.” The roadmap grows in three stages — first the best vocabulary tool for Singapore primary schools, then a better experience for parents who don't speak Chinese, then beyond Singapore primary to new learners and exams.

Each stage has a clear value proposition, the features that deliver it, and the features that can make money — so I can decide what to build next, not just what to build.

03

iOS: closing the loop

September 2026
Going native

Closing the independent-study loop

What parents asked for most was independent study: a child who can prepare for tingxie without a parent reading every word. That only works when the child can learn, test themselves, see the result and revise what they got wrong — on their own.

In the web beta, children listened on a laptop and wrote on paper. Tingxie is a writing test, so the most important moment — writing the answer — happened outside the product, where no one could check it.

That's why I took the product native. On iOS, children write Hanzi with a finger or Apple Pencil and type Pinyin with tone marks, right in the test. The app checks each character — "Not quite, try 5 strokes" — and they can see right away which words to revise. Learn, test, check, revise — the whole loop now happens in one place, by the child.

Design system

A small system, built for two scripts

Even as a one-person team, I'm building TingXie on a design system: semantic colour tokens, a UI type ramp, and components with documented hierarchy, sizes, states and usage rules. Tokens, buttons and inputs already run in the iOS app and on the web; the rest rolls out as the design settles, so I'm not hard-coding decisions that are still changing.

The part I care most about is the language type. Pinyin and Hanzi each get their own ramp, and Hanzi is set in a Kai typeface — the regular script children learn to write at school — so the characters they read match the strokes they practise.
