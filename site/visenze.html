Objective

“Design the advanced feature for recommendations”

Recommendations (not “Recommendation” 🙂) is ViSenze’s most popular feature for online retailers to contextually recommend products within end users’ buying journey. To strengthen its proposition in the market, the team planned to introduce an advanced ‘Customization’ feature. With this feature, our client can fully control what’s being recommended — the products (could specify brand, price, color, pattern, etc), the order of display, and the number of recommended products.

Functionally, what to build is already defined as we are enabling backend capability to the frontend. Then how should we form the experience? I was in charge of shaping the experience of it.

Challenge

“How much complexity will expert users accept?”

We were adding an advanced customization feature, so some complexity was unavoidable. The question was how much of it users would feel comfortable with.

Our users are digital merchandisers professionally trained in setting up strategies to enhance product visibility. If the experience is overly explanatory, it will slow down their workflow. If it is too complex, they won't use it. We needed to find the level that felt easy enough for experts.

Leading the team

Leading two designers through the project

I ran this project as a design sprint with two designers on my team. They built the research prototypes and produced the visual design; I owned the UX flow and made the final call on design quality.

When the first round of research came back poorly, I stepped in and reworked the flow myself — the shift from rules to intent that you'll see below. On visual design, the designers drove the work and I reviewed, refined and signed it off.

Approach

I took a look at industry leaders’ experience first to understand terminology, flows, patterns. It seems like there’s no common pattern, and interestingly, our users mentioned that they had to go through a steep learning curve to get used to each tool. That means, we could be a standard of good user experience.

So I did 3 rounds of user testing for this project to figure out an ‘easy enough’ user experience. Each session gave me great hints to further improve. Each round of test was conducted in a similar format: we gave users a set of tasks, and they needed to talk aloud while running the task.

Round 1

Failed hard

Luckily we tested this! When I walked through the flow with the working team, I received positive feedback so I was somewhat confident in the flow. The testing turned out that no one could clearly understand the UI. Link to the prototype ↗

“Need to shift from rule base to human intent”

The language used in the screen was showing the ‘mechanism’ of the rule, not the user intent. It took a while to understand when to choose ‘Boost product’; only when we gave a hint that you may want to promote certain products over the rest, users could choose ‘boost’. What’s worse was the second level option: no one got what ‘input output match’ was, although it was presented with explanation. Conceptual explanation doesn’t help.

Round 2

Provide a guide for setting up the rule

Learning from the research, I shifted the experience to be ‘intent driven’. Users need to choose what they want to do, and specify when and how. Also, to give some more guidance for users to set up the rule, I added ‘presets’ — a collection of rules frequently asked for by our customers.

The result was much better! And this time the testers also gave great inputs:

  • Everyone loved frequently used rules! They want to see frequently used rules by default, as this would be the guide to set up their rule.
  • Users want to emphasize when to trigger the rule; in the current UI, it’s hidden.
  • When setting up the details of the rule, no one understood ‘match with’.
Round 3

Don’t make users start from scratch

I applied the lessons and did quick sessions with a slightly changed prototype. I opened up the frequently used rules and made ‘add if’ a necessary step to specify ‘when’ to apply these rules. This worked, but users gave us a great insight that shifted the mental model: start the rule setting from frequently used rules and modify if they want, so that users won’t start rule creation from scratch. That will shorten the time to set up the rule.

Result & lesson

We released this feature and received great improvement in our clients’ revenue.

  • EyeBuyDirect saw an increase of $1.2 million in its annual revenue on mobile alone, and a 7% increase in conversions after implementing ViSenze Smart Recommendations.
  • Zalora saw an engagement rate more than 10% higher and a 15% increase in average order value.

This project reminded me of the importance of early testing. If we hadn’t tested, the product wouldn’t have been so well received. Early testing revealed that we were in danger of creating a product in a mechanical way — users are experts in the industry, but not in the system. They don’t have a specific ‘rule’ in their mind yet. Showing the guiding rules and letting them preview the results and modify from there is a much easier way. And the early test enabled us to quickly change the notion of the design.
