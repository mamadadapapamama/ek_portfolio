Brief

I've lived abroad for about fifteen years, and I always wanted a home back in Korea. I renovated an old house in Seogwipo, Jeju and have hosted it since 2023. I also bought a plot near the sea in Gujwa with three old stone houses, and turned it into two new guest homes — as my own builder, while living and working in Singapore.

Doing it remotely meant every decision had to work without me there: the space, the guest journey, and the tools I use to run it.

Designing the space

Keep what makes it Jeju

The plot came with three old stone houses. Two crossed the boundary line and had to come down. From the third, I kept the two stone walls you see from the lane and built around them, so the new homes still belong to the village.

The ground had sunk and was mostly sand, so I filled it with stone, raised the site and lifted the foundation 60 cm. I ran the build myself, with no general contractor: demolition and foundations while I was in Jeju, the steel frame arranged from Singapore, then back on site to take every remaining trade to completion in about two months.

Inside, I designed for how guests actually stay — a week or a month, often as a family: high pitched ceilings, a loft for kids, a big table to work and eat at, and windows framing the stone walls outside.

Designing the service

Hosting three homes from another country

I can't hand over keys or answer the door, so I designed the guest journey to run on its own and built my own tools to run it.

  1. 01A host dashboard I built. Bookings from direct guests and two rental platforms, notes and settlements across all three homes in one view — because no single platform showed me everything.
  2. 02A self-serve guest journey. Contactless check-in with a digital lock. Check-in, house guides and check-out messages written once in Notion, so every guest gets the same clear answers without waiting on me.
  3. 03One familiar channel. Guests reach me on KakaoTalk, the app every Korean guest already uses — not a platform inbox.
What guests say

Designed for longer stays, felt by families

Many guests stay one to two weeks, often with young children. Their reviews keep returning to the same things I designed for: a home ready for kids, a view to slow down with, a village to belong to, and a host who answers fast — even from abroad.

What I learned

Where a global platform meets local rules

In 2024 I listed my one home at the time on Airbnb. It received three bookings, including a month-long stay — rated 5.0, with a 100% response rate. Demand wasn't the problem. Then, in late 2024, the rules changed: listing a home in Korea now requires an accommodation business licence, and in Jeju the licence types — hotels, pensions with commercial-scale facilities, rural homestays — don't fit an owner with a second home who lives somewhere else. Short one- or two-night stays also don't work when the host is overseas.

That is the irony. A home that would otherwise sit empty while its owner lives abroad or in another city is close to what Airbnb was built for: people sharing space they already have. In Korea, it is exactly the case the rules leave out.

So I took the listing down before the new rules took effect and moved to local mid-term rental platforms with a six-night minimum, where that first home now runs at over 90% occupancy. I'd still rather reach Airbnb's global guests — but the path for hosts like me isn't there yet.

That gap is what I find most interesting as a designer: a global product working well in one market and stalling in another, not because of the interface, but because of local regulation, local habits and local platforms. I've seen it from the host's side, and I'd love to work on it from the inside.
